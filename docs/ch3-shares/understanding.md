# Two values, two jobs

A **share** is a small ownership unit in a company. Its owner is a **shareholder**. For this chapter, each share has a fixed **face value** (also nominal, printed or par value) and a **market value**, the price paid in a transaction.

Imagine a share with **₹100 printed on it** being sold for **₹120**. You pay ₹120 to buy it. If the declared dividend is 8%, the dividend calculation starts from the printed ₹100, giving ₹8 per year.

| Quantity | Meaning | Our example |
|---|---|---|
| Face value F | Base for declared dividend | ₹100 |
| Market value M | Price of one share | ₹120 |
| Number n | Count of shares owned | 10 |
| Investment I | Money spent acquiring them | 10 × 120 = ₹1,200 |
| Total face value nF | Printed value of the holding | 10 × 100 = ₹1,000 |

**Investment and total face value are different quantities.** They coincide only when bought at par.

## Skill: decode the quotation

Read “350, ₹20 shares” as **350 shares, face value ₹20 each**. A “₹20 share” does not tell you its current buying price.

| Recognition clue | Market price | Example |
|---|---|---|
| At par | $M=F$ | ₹50 share at par → ₹50 |
| At ₹7 premium | $M=F+7$ | ₹20 share → ₹27 |
| At ₹10 discount | $M=F-10$ | ₹60 share → ₹50 |
| At 20% premium | $M=F(1+20/100)$ | ₹50 share → ₹60 |
| At 20% discount | $M=F(1-20/100)$ | ₹50 share → ₹40 |
| Quoted at ₹38.50 | $M=38.50$ | Face value can be ₹40; buy at ₹38.50 |

**Rupees and percentages are distinct variations.** ₹20 premium and 20% premium coincide for F = ₹100, but not for F = ₹50.

### Worked example W01 — three quotations (source p. 25)

1. 350 ₹20 shares at ₹7 premium: M = 20 + 7 = ₹27; cost = 350 × 27 = **₹9,450**.
2. 275 ₹60 shares at ₹10 discount: M = 60 − 10 = ₹50; cost = 275 × 50 = **₹13,750**.
3. 50 ₹40 shares quoted at ₹38.50: M is already given; cost = 50 × 38.50 = **₹1,925**.

The calculation is the same multiplication, but the language-to-price transformation changes. All three belong in the variation map.

## Skill: move between money and share count

$$I=nM\qquad n=\frac{I}{M}\qquad M=\frac{I}{n}$$

Think in units: **shares × rupees/share = rupees**. To recover a share count, divide money by the price of one share.

### Worked example W02 — investment to income (source p. 25)

Rakhee invests ₹12,500 in ₹50 shares costing ₹62.50, with a 6% dividend.

1. **Buying:** n = 12500/62.50 = 200 shares. Use M because this is money spent.
2. **One-share dividend:** d = 6% of ₹50 = ₹3. Use F because this is dividend.
3. **Holding’s dividend:** D = 200 × ₹3 = **₹600 annually**.

The shortcut “6% of ₹12,500” would give ₹750 and is wrong: ₹12,500 is market cost, not the nominal value of the holding.

## Common errors and checks

- Dividing investment by face value: check whether the shares were at par.
- Adding a market quote to face value: “quoted at” already supplies M.
- Reading 20% discount as paying 20%: you pay 80% of face value.
- Accepting a fractional count without comment: inspect the wording and arithmetic; do not round silently.
