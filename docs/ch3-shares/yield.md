# How much income for each rupee invested?

A ₹100 share paying 12% gives ₹12 income. If bought for ₹150, ₹12 is only 8% of the buyer’s ₹150. If bought for ₹80, the same ₹12 is 15% of the cost.

$$y=\frac{D}{I}\times100=\frac{Fr}{M}$$

Why does the share count disappear? Since D = nFr/100 and I = nM, the n cancels. This is why we can compare companies without choosing an investment amount first.

## The three-way relationship

$$yM=rF\qquad M=\frac{rF}{y}\qquad r=\frac{yM}{F}$$

r and y are numerical percentages. For example, use r = 12 and y = 8, consistently.

| Recognition clue | Unknown | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage income / return / yield | y | Fr/M |
| “What price did he pay to earn 10%?” | M | Fr/y |
| “What rate does the company declare?” | r | yM/F |
| Investment and annual dividend known | M | First n = D/d, then M = I/n |

## Worked example W03 — the denominator (p. 26)

600 ₹100 shares at ₹20 premium pay 15%.

M = ₹120; I = 600 × 120 = ₹72,000; D = 600 × 15 = ₹9,000.

$$y=\frac{9000}{72000}\times100=12.5\%$$

Check without all 600 shares: 15/120 × 100 = 12.5%. The declared 15% is not the buyer’s 12.5% return.

## Worked example W09 — reverse yield, then target income (p. 29)

9% ₹100 shares yield 7.5%.

1. d = ₹9.
2. 7.5% of M equals ₹9, so M = 9 × 100/7.5 = **₹120**.
3. For ₹630 income, n = 630/9 = **70 shares**.
4. Investment = 70 × 120 = **₹8,400**.

For W07’s remaining part: F = ₹110, r = 15, y = 10; M = 110 × 15/10 = **₹165**.

## Reverse rate — original mini-example

A ₹50 share costs ₹80 and yields 5%. Its annual income per share is 5% of ₹80 = ₹4. Thus declared dividend rate = 4/50 × 100 = **8%**. The different unknown makes this distinct from finding market price.

## Worked example W10 — compare yields (pp. 29–30)

| Choice | Dividend per share | Market price | Yield |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 12% ₹100 share at ₹120 | ₹12 | ₹120 | 10% |
| 8% ₹100 share at ₹90 | ₹8 | ₹90 | 8.888…% |

The first offers the higher dividend yield. In **B12** a lower declared dividend rate actually wins: 16% at ₹80 yields 20%, while 20% at ₹120 yields only 16⅔%.

For a known equal investment, convert the yield difference into money. In **B13**, ₹26,400 buys 220 shares earning ₹5 each in the first company and 200 shares earning ₹6 in the second: annual incomes ₹1,100 and ₹1,200; difference **₹100**.

## Reasoning and rounding variations

For positive dividend rate, compare yield y with dividend rate r:

- **y = r:** at par.
- **y is less than r:** market price is above face value.
- **y is greater than r:** market price is below face value.

For **B09**, calculate 1500/140 = 10.714…% before rounding to **11%**. Do not replace 10.714… with 11 in intermediate calculations.
