# Follow the money and keep the two incomes separate

A transaction has three prices that may differ: **original purchase price**, **sale price**, and **new company’s purchase price**. Face values are still used only for dividend calculations.

| Stage | Calculate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Before sale | nOld and Dold = nOld dOld | Establish the old annual income |
| Sale | S = nSold × sale price | Find cash available |
| New purchase | nNew = S/new market price | Cash buys a new number of shares |
| After purchase | Dnew = nNew dNew | Apply the new dividend |
| Comparison | Dnew − Dold | Report increase or decrease |

If only part is sold, compare the replaced part’s income or explicitly include the retained holding on both sides. Never discard retained shares silently.

## Worked example W05 — partial sale gain (pp. 26–27)

₹9,000 buys 60 shares at ₹150. Each ₹100 share pays 6%, giving ₹360 annual income before sale. Half the holding means **30 shares** sold at ₹200.

Capital gain = 30 × (200 − 150) = **₹1,500**.

The cost deducted is ₹4,500 for the **30 shares sold**, not the entire ₹9,000 holding. Their ₹6,000 sale proceeds are not the same as ₹1,500 profit. Independent transfer: **P02**.

## Worked example W11 — reinvest sale proceeds (p. 30)

Old holding: 60 ₹15 shares paying 12%. Sell at ₹21 and buy ₹6 shares at ₹9 paying 8%.

1. Old income = 60 × 15 × .12 = **₹108**.
2. Sale cash = 60 × 21 = **₹1,260**.
3. New shares = 1260/9 = **140**.
4. New dividend per share = 6 × .08 = **₹0.48**.
5. New income = 140 × .48 = **₹67.20**.
6. Change = 67.20 − 108 = **−₹40.80**, a decrease.

More shares do not necessarily mean more income: the face value and dividend per share also changed.

## Worked example W12 — include the dividend only when told (pp. 30–31)

₹8,000 buys 100 shares at ₹80; each ₹100 share pays 7%. Dividend = ₹700. Sell at ₹75, then reinvest **sale proceeds including dividend** in 18% ₹25 shares at ₹41.

New capital = 100 × 75 + 700 = **₹8,200**, not ₹7,500.

New shares = 8200/41 = 200. New income = 200 × 25 × .18 = **₹900**.

| Question being asked | Calculation | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Income increase | 900 − 700 | ₹200 per year |
| Increase as % of original capital | 200/8000 × 100 | 2.5% of original capital |
| Old/new income as % of original capital | 700/8000 and 900/8000 | 8.75% and 11.25% |
| Difference between those rates | 11.25 − 8.75 | 2.5 percentage points |
| Relative percentage growth in income | 200/700 × 100 | 28.571…% |

The textbook asks for the original-capital denominator. Do not replace it with relative income growth. Independent transfer: **P03**.

## Partial holdings and total profit

**Cash target — T09.** If sale proceeds must be ₹8,400 at ₹140 per share, sell 60 shares. Subtract 60 from the original holding, then calculate future income on the remainder. Cash raised is not a profit target.

**Fraction sold — T16.** If 80% is sold, 20% remains. Calculate future annual dividend on that remaining 20%. This says nothing about the dividend already earned before selling.

**Total earnings — T13.** When asked for profit **including dividend**, compute capital gain first, then add the received dividend. Do not add the entire sale proceeds as profit.

## Reverse reinvestment: let one old share do the work

If the old count n is unknown, calculate the income change generated by the sale of **one old share**:

$$\text{change per old share}=\frac{M_{sell}}{M_{new}}d_{new}-d_{old}$$

Then multiply by n and equate to the stated **signed** income change.

### Original worked example

Old dividend per share is ₹4; sell each old share at ₹60. New shares cost ₹20 and pay ₹2 each. One old share buys three new shares, generating ₹6, so income increases by ₹2 per old share. An annual increase of ₹300 requires **150 old shares**.

If the original purchase price was ₹50, the original investment was 150 × 50 = **₹7,500**. This last step is essential when the question asks for capital, not count.

For a decrease, keep the sign consistent. In **T17**, each old share’s ₹18 proceeds produce ₹1.44 new income, below the old ₹1.60. The change is a **decrease** of ₹0.16 per old share. “Change ₹120” must therefore mean a decrease.

## Wording traps

**T01g:** successive percentages use different bases. ₹50 face value at 20% discount costs ₹40; a 25% gain on cost gives ₹50 selling price. The gain is 25% of cost, not 25 − 20 = 5%.

**T03:** “shares worth ₹72,000” does not specify whether “worth” means nominal value or sale proceeds. The worked answer in our bank explicitly assumes nominal value and also shows the alternative. Treat this as an interpretation exercise, not an unqualified one-answer drill.
