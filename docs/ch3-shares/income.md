# Start with one share

If a share’s face value is F and its declared dividend is r%, then its dividend per year is:

$$d=\frac{Fr}{100}\qquad D=nd$$

Here r is the number 7.5 when the rate is 7.5%. Write 7.5/100 once. Do not divide by 100 twice.

**Dividend rate** is a percentage; **dividend income** is a rupee amount. The textbook sometimes calls annual dividend “profit” or “return.” Keep it separate from the profit made when selling a share at a higher price.

## Recognition map

| Wanted | Given | Method | Distinct variation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dividend per share | F, r | d = Fr/100 | One-share income |
| Annual income | n, F, r | D = nd | Direct income |
| Annual income | I, M, F, r | n = I/M, then D = nd | Linked buying-and-income chain |
| Required shares | Desired D, F, r | n = D/d | Reverse income |
| Required investment | Desired D and M | I = (D/d)M | Reverse income plus buying |
| Dividend rate | D, n, F | r = 100D/(nF) | Different unknown |
| Extra shares | Increase ΔD, F, r | extra n = ΔD/d | Increment rather than total |
| Extra investment | Increase ΔD and M | extra I = (ΔD/d)M | Increment plus buying |

## Worked example W04 — find the declared rate (p. 26)

500 shares of face value ₹10 give dividend ₹400.

Total face value = 500 × 10 = ₹5,000. Therefore:

$$r=\frac{400}{500\times10}\times100=8$$

**Dividend rate = 8%.** The source solution describes ₹5,000 as “sum invested.” That label is unjustified without market price. It is the total face value, which is the correct denominator for dividend rate.

## Worked example W06 — “increase by” (p. 27)

62 ₹100 shares cost ₹132 and pay 7.5%.

- Investment: 62 × 132 = **₹8,184**.
- Dividend per share: 7.5% of ₹100 = **₹7.50**.
- Current income: 62 × 7.5 = **₹465**.
- For **₹150 more** income: extra shares = 150/7.5 = **20**.

If the question had said “increase income **to** ₹600,” the extra income would be 600 − 465 = ₹135, requiring 18 additional shares. This is an original wording contrast, not another source question.

If extra money is requested, continue: extra shares × M. **T05** tests this extra stage.

## Worked example W07 — company versus shareholder (p. 27)

A company has 4,000 ₹110 shares and declares 15%. One investor holds 88 shares.

One-share dividend = 110 × .15 = ₹16.50.

| Whose income? | Share count | Dividend |
|---|---:|---:|
| All shareholders together | 4,000 | 4000 × 16.5 = ₹66,000 |
| This investor | 88 | 88 × 16.5 = ₹1,452 |

The source’s third part asks the purchase price for a 10% yield; that reverse calculation is taught in [Yield](./yield.md).

## Two variations that should not be skipped

**Half-yearly dividend — B01c (p. 33).** 100 shares of ₹120 at par give **10% each half-year**. One payment = 100 × 120 × .10 = ₹1,200. Two payments = **₹2,400 annually**. This does not mean compound interest: no reinvestment is stated. An annual rate paid in two instalments would be a different statement.

**Two purchase batches — T01b (p. 34).** 100 ₹100 shares bought at ₹80 and another 100 bought at ₹120 both receive the same 10% declared dividend. Each share earns ₹10; combined income is **₹2,000**. Purchase prices affect yield, not dividend per share.
